Responsible AI Systems

Ethical and Moral Foundations for Artificial Intelligence Adoption

Explore the foundational human principles, moral dilemmas, and psychological biases that shape how we evaluate and adopt artificial intelligence.

Dr. Ian McCullough, Johns Hopkins University

The Three Pillars

Setting the stage for responsible AI evaluation.

Ethics

  • Principles or guidelines that govern conduct within a particular context.
  • Derived from theories, philosophy, and professional standards.
  • Deals with questions about what is right and wrong.

Values

  • Individual or collective beliefs about what is important, desirable, and worthwhile.
  • Reflect personal or cultural priorities.
  • Inform many of the decisions and behaviors that people do.

Morality

  • Principles or rules of behavior societies believe are right or wrong.
  • Focused on practical everyday conduct.
  • Often carries strong emotional or social component.

These three concepts—slightly different but deeply connected—are the basis for thinking about responsible artificial intelligence.

"When we're adopting artificial intelligence solutions, how do they support or come into conflict with some of our ethics, values, and morality?"

Let's explore these foundations through a series of classic moral dilemmas.

The Trolley Problem Series

These dilemmas explore the needs of the many vs. the needs of the few. What is your moral responsibility to take action or not take action?

Variation 1: The Original Dilemma

Save One vs. Save Five

Setup: A trolley is hurtling down the tracks toward five tied-up people. You can cut the ropes to free one person in front of you.

Choice: Free the one person (and the five definitely die), or ignore the one person (train derails, saving the five).

What would you do?

Variation 2: Self-Sacrifice

Personal Cost

Setup: Same scenario, but you can save the person in front of you by jumping in front of the train.

Choice: Sacrifice yourself to save everyone else.

Would you sacrifice yourself?

Variation 3: The Switch

Active Harm vs. Passive Outcome

Setup: The train is headed for five people. There is a switch you can pull to divert the train to a track with only one person.

Question: You're responsible for killing the one person in order to save the other five. What would you do?

Hippocratic Oath Connection

"First, do no harm". You can't control why those people are tied to the tracks. But if you pull the switch, you have actually been culpable in that person's harm.

AI Implication: As this gets implemented in AI, we may wonder if that's actually a good moral foundation to stand on when looking at AI adoption in healthcare and medicine.

Variation 4: The Fat Man on the Bridge

Physical Action

Setup: You are on a bridge. A large man is on the bridge. Five people are on the track below.

Choice: Push the man off the bridge to save the five people.

"This was possibly my favorite one because it's just so absurd".

Variation 5: The Child Variation

Value of Life

Setup: Go back to the original problem, but the person on the track is a child.

Question: Would you be more willing to untie the child even at the expense of the five? Are certain lives more valuable than others?

Key Insights:

  • There's no universal right or wrong. Slight alterations change people's responses.
  • It opens up an aspect of human psychology that we have bias towards ethics and morality under different conditions.
  • It reveals where we're willing to take risk or not take risk.
Why This Matters for AI: All we're discussing about AI ethics is rooted in centuries of questions about human morality. We need to understand where our human biases are so that we can compare AI on an "apples to apples" basis.

Loss Aversion

Humans are more afraid to lose than they are willing to risk gain.

  • A loss is considered much worse than an equivalent potential gain.
  • This is based on empirical evidence.
  • Your potential gain has to be much higher than your potential loss in order for most people to take action.
The $5 Test: Would you risk $5 at the possibility of losing $5? Most people say no. (Though this doesn't hold for everyone—some are thrilled by gambling).

Value Function Curve

Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains feel good.

Gain vs. Loss Framing: The Decision Experiment

Your company is facing potential financial loss. You are the executive. Which options do you choose?

Scenario 1: The Gain Frame

Option A: Certain Gain

Implement a cost-saving measure that will definitely save $500,000.

Option B: Risky Gain

Implement a risky project. 50% chance of saving $1,000,000 and 50% chance of saving nothing.

Scenario 2: The Loss Frame

Option C: Certain Loss

Implement a penalty that will definitely cost $500,000.

Option D: Risky Loss

Implement a risky legal strategy. 50% chance of costing $1,000,000 and 50% chance of costing nothing.

The Reveal

Most commonly people answer Option A and Option D.

The Math: All four options have identical expected values of $500,000. Depending on your status quo benchmark, these are four equivalent situations.

Gain Frame Behavior: People don't like the idea of losing a million dollars, so they prefer certainty (Option A).
Loss Frame Behavior: People are more in favor of the possibility of losing nothing, so they take the risk (Option D).

"Because we are more averse to loss than we are to gain."

AI Investment Through the Lens of Loss Aversion

Applying the same mathematical framing directly to AI adoption decisions.

Typical Choice
Option A: Gain Frame / No AI
Cost Cutting — Safe Choice

Cut overhead costs and internal R&D. Saves $500,000.

Option B: Gain Frame / AI
AI Investment — Risky

Implement an AI program. 50% chance to save $1,000,000, 50% chance to save nothing.

Option C: Loss Frame / AI
AI Cost — Certain Loss

Make an AI investment that will cost the company $500,000.

Typical Choice
Option D: Loss Frame / No AI
M&A — Risky Alternative

Pursue a merger acquisition. 50% chance to cost $1,000,000, 50% chance to cost nothing.

The Bias Against AI: Executives plagued by loss aversion are more likely to cut overhead instead of actually investing in AI programs, which might have a longer chance of growth in the long run.

The Risk Paradox: People are less likely to make that AI investment, and go through crazy merger acquisitions that are more risky because of that loss aversion where it doesn't seem like as negative a scenario.

Same math. Different frame. Opposite treatment of AI.

Fast Thinking

Mental shortcuts that allow fast decisions, especially in complex and uncertain situations.

If we understand loss aversion, we know those shortcuts are not necessarily optimal solutions. Bias may disfavor adoption of AI.

— Ref: Daniel Kahneman (Nobel Laureate), "Thinking, Fast and Slow".

Slow Thinking

Deliberate analysis and expected value calculation. More accurate, but costly in effort.

"Understanding where the potential bias is allows us to make better informed decisions."

Instead of looking at expected value—what decisions pay out in the long run—we have greater aversion to loss.

When Intuition Works

Experienced art collectors can just look at a piece and know whether it's fake. Intuition and fast thinking is sometimes positive.

— Ref: Malcolm Gladwell, "Blink"

Organizational Reality

This bias isn't just for the decision-maker. It's also how they justify the potential gain or loss to their boss for bonuses, promotions, and avoiding layoffs.

The Dual Nature of AI and Bias

Path 1: Inherit Bias

AI can also adopt similar bias when trained by humans.

Trained on biased historical data, it reinforces existing patterns and fallible mental shortcuts.

Path 2: Expose Bias

Or it can expose the bias and allow us to look at it in a more objective sense.

Reveals patterns humans cannot see objectively, enabling better, less biased decision-making.

Which path depends on how we design, train, and deploy AI systems.

Ethical Frameworks

These formal frameworks name the moral reasoning the trolley problems implicitly test.

Utilitarianism

Maximize overall good; greatest benefit for greatest number.

Trolley: The Switch (save 5)

AI: Optimize aggregate outcomes.

Deontology

Right/wrong based on rules/duties, regardless of outcome.

Trolley: Hippocratic Oath

AI: Rule-based governance.

Virtue Ethics

Focus on character/intent of the decision-maker.

Trolley: Fat Man on Bridge

AI: Reflect virtuous qualities.

Care Ethics

Prioritizes relationships, context, and vulnerability.

Trolley: The Child Variation

AI: Protect the vulnerable.

Beyond Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is one of many biases that affect AI adoption decisions.

Status Quo Bias

Preference for current state over change (cost-cutting over AI).

Anchoring Bias

Over-relying on first info encountered (initial AI cost estimates).

Confirmation Bias

Seeking info confirming existing beliefs (distrusting AI).

Availability Heuristic

Overweighting vivid examples (one high-profile AI failure).

Actionable Responsible AI Principles

Translating the ethical foundations into actionable guidelines.

Fairness

Test for bias; ensure AI doesn't disadvantage groups.

Transparency

Make decisions understandable to affected parties.

Accountability

Maintain human oversight for high-stakes decisions.

Safety

Apply "do no harm" while weighing cost of inaction.

Autonomy

Preserve human agency; avoid framing manipulation.

Key Takeaways

"Understand human bias first — then evaluate AI on an apples to apples basis."

Ethics, values, and morality are distinct but interconnected foundations for AI.

Trolley problems reveal human moral intuitions shift with context and framing.

The Hippocratic Oath applies to AI, but may be insufficient when inaction causes harm.

Loss aversion proves humans fear losses roughly twice as much as equivalent gains.

Gain and loss framing causes inconsistent decisions even with identical expected values.

Executives prefer cutting overhead over investing in AI due to loss aversion.

Fast thinking (heuristics) has value, but bias awareness improves decisions.

AI has a dual nature: it can inherit human bias or expose it depending on design.

Understanding human bias is a PREREQUISITE for evaluating AI bias.

Ethics, values, and morality are the foundation. Loss aversion explains the resistance. Understanding both is the prerequisite for responsible AI adoption.
