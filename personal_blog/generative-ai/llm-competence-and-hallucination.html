LLM Competence and Hallucination

When AI Gets It Right, When AI Makes It Up, and How to Tell the Difference

Based on lectures by Dr. Ian McCullough, Johns Hopkins University

Machine learning based generative AI introduces probability and stochasticity, creating room for error. We categorize these errors into two distinct types.

Competence Failures

The model breaks structural rules of language or fails at functional logic and reasoning.

Hallucinations

The model fabricates plausible-sounding but entirely incorrect information that does not exist.

Let's start by understanding what makes a model competent

What Makes a Model Competent?

Accuracy

Is it the right answer?

Example: "Capital of France = Paris" (Not Lyon or Nice due to recent news bias).

Understanding

Is it consistent with human understanding?

Example: Discussing carbon emissions and renewable energy for "climate change" without going off-topic.

Consistency

Will it give the same answer if asked multiple times?

Generalizability

Can outputs apply to other problems or areas?

Formal Competence Skills

"A competent model will not make these types of errors. The structural foundation of language."

1. Phonology

Rules governing what is a valid word form.

Correct "blick" — valid English word form structure
Incorrect "bnick" — violates phonological rules

2. Morphology

Rules governing valid language combinations.

Correct "Lady Gaga-esque-ness"
Incorrect "Lady Gaga-ness-esque"

3. Lexical Semantics

Where parts of speech or word meanings occur.

Correct "Coffee with cream and sugar"
Incorrect "Coffee with cream and red"

4. Syntax

Grammatical structure and agreement.

Correct "The key to the cabinets is on the table"
Incorrect "The key to the cabinets are on the table"

Many of these can be enforced with symbolic AI or rules applied to the output.

Functional Competence Skills

Higher-order reasoning. "This is where it's a lot more difficult. Errors here are grammatically perfect but logically wrong."

Formal Reasoning

Logic, math, and planning capabilities.

✓ 14 birds - 3 + 1 = 12 birds
✗ 14 birds - 3 + 1 = 11 birds

A model failing basic arithmetic demonstrates a formal reasoning error, proving it lacks functional competence despite understanding the sentence structure.

World Knowledge

Physical realities and common sense.

✓ Trophy doesn't fit because suitcase is too small
✗ Trophy doesn't fit because trophy is too small

Understanding how physical objects relate to each other (size constraints) requires real-world knowledge beyond word associations.

Situational Modeling

Maintaining an accurate mental state of a scenario.

✓ Sally owns a dog → the dog is black
✗ Sally doesn't own a dog → the dog is black

If the premise establishes that no dog exists, proceeding to describe the non-existent dog is a failure of situational modeling.

Social Reasoning

Theory of mind and understanding perspectives.

✓ Lou thinks toy is in the box
✗ Lou thinks toy is in the closet

Requires tracking what different agents know independently of what the "narrator" (or model) knows to be true.

Understanding different types of competence is important if you want to test and validate that your model is performing reliably.

Theory of Mind — The Lou and Bo Test

Understanding that different people might have different perspectives.

In Psychology:

Develops around age 4-5. Before this, a child looking into the back of a dollhouse assumes someone in front can see the same hidden furniture.

In AI:

Tests whether the model can track what different agents know within a scenario.

Scenario:

Lou puts the toy in the box and leaves. Bo secretly moves the toy to the closet.

✓

Correct Reasoning

"Lou thinks the toy is in the box." Lou doesn't know Bo moved it.

✗

Incorrect Reasoning

"Lou thinks the toy is in the closet." Lou has no perspective to know what Bo did, even though that's where the object actually is.

Hallucination: Fabrication in Action

"Plausible sounding information that's entirely made up." Click cards to reveal the generated fiction.

John Doe

Fabricated Historical Figure

Prompt: "Tell me about the contributions of John Doe to the American Revolution."
"John Doe was a key figure... known for his strategic prowess during the Battle of Bunker Hill and his diplomatic efforts with France."

Reality: John Doe is a fabricated figure with no historical reality.

Dr. Jane Smith

Fabricated Scientist & Particle

Prompt: "What are some recent discoveries made by Dr. Jane Smith in quantum mechanics?"
"Jane Smith recently discovered a new particle called the quantum photon, which has revolutionized our understanding..."

Reality: Fictitious scientist and entirely invented scientific concept.

Zetacode

Fabricated Programming Language

Prompt: "Explain the new programming language Zetacode developed by Google."
"Zetacode is a new language developed by Google that combines the simplicity of Python with the performance of C++..."

Reality: Completely fabricated language with no Google development.

Panacea X

Fabricated Medical Drug

Prompt: "What are the benefits of the new drug Panacea X for treating diabetes?"
"Panacea X is a breakthrough drug offering a 90% reduction in blood sugar levels... it works by activating a unique enzyme."

Reality: Fictitious drug with entirely fabricated medical claims.

Why it happens:

"Looking at former historical situations or context or figures... the model can take a name and generate a really good, plausible sounding story without any facts behind it."

Why Hallucination Happens

"Probability and stochasticity create room for error."

1

Pattern Matching, Not Fact Retrieval

LLMs generate text by predicting the next probable token based on statistical patterns, not retrieving true facts.

2

Gap-Filling with Plausible Text

When asked about something not in training data, it fills gaps using the nearest patterns (e.g., historical military tone for "John Doe").

3

No Internal "I Don't Know" Signal

The model generates all text with the same confident tone. It cannot distinguish its own knowledge from its guessing.

Prompt Engineering Guardrails

"Always ask in the prompt to cite references."

The Lecturer's Workflow

  1. Ask AI for examples of fabrication
  2. Require citations in APA format
  3. Verify cited references actually exist
  4. Use validated examples

This constrains the AI to look for actual sources before making something up. Other techniques include:

  • Instructing: "Say 'I don't know' if unsure"
  • Providing few-shot examples
  • Using chain-of-thought reasoning
  • Constraining output formats

Minimizing Hallucination

Six comprehensive strategies to build more reliable AI systems.

Highest Impact

1. High-Quality Data

"The highest gains come from better data, not model tuning."

2. Fine-Tuning

"Fine-tune with specific objectives in mind."

Reduces hallucination in specific tasks you use it for.

3. Prompt Engineering

"Cite my references in APA format."

Constrain the AI to look for real sources.

4. Post-Processing

"Run through formal verification — symbolic AI."

First-order logic to ensure referential integrity.

5. Human in the Loop

"Humans checking it — but humans are prone to errors."

⚠ Humans hallucinate too!

6. Regular Updates

"Regular updates and training to the model and data."

Keep information current and accurate.

Data Quality Lever

"I've always achieved the highest gains not by tuning the model, but by providing better quality data."

Correct Right facts, no errors
Consistent No contradictions
Complete No important gaps
Current Up to date
Representative Covers domains

More attention to correct data = better model, less hallucination.

Competence vs. Hallucination

Competence

Definition

Model produces accurate, understanding, consistent, generalizable outputs.

Formal Dimension

Phonology, morphology, lexical semantics, syntax.

Functional Dimension

Reasoning, world knowledge, situational modeling, theory of mind.

How to Enforce

Symbolic AI and rules applied to output for formal competence. Structured structured evaluations across dimensions for testing.

Hallucination

Definition

Model produces incorrect, fabricated, inconsistent, unreliable creative outputs.

Fabrication

Generating plausible but entirely made-up information (e.g., John Doe in the Revolution).

Inconsistency

Contradicting itself or mixing up facts.

How to Mitigate

Citation requests, higher quality training data, fine-tuning, RAG, and human review.

"A model can be formally competent (perfect grammar) while still hallucinating (making up facts)."

The Bottom Line

Competence and hallucination represent two sides of the same coin in machine learning based generative AI. Whenever we allow probability and stochasticity into a model, there is room for error.

Formal competence ensures the model follows the structural rules of language. Functional competence tests deeper reasoning and understanding. Hallucination — plausible-sounding but entirely fabricated information — remains one of the greatest challenges.

But high-quality data, prompt engineering, symbolic AI, and human review work together to build reliable systems. The highest gains always come from better data, not better models. Generative AI is still very useful, but there are things we can do to create guardrails and prevent mistakes.

Dr. Ian McCullough, Johns Hopkins University
