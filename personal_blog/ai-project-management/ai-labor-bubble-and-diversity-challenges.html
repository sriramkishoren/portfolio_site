The AI Labor Bubble

How COVID Ignited an Unprecedented Demand for AI Talent

During the COVID pandemic, people worldwide tried to socially distance, work from home, and not go into the office. This necessitated a massive rise in communications and IT technology to enable remote work.

A large early investment in this technology got people comfortable with AI, giving organizations the necessary resources to make significant innovations.

This was quickly followed by the artificial intelligence labor bubble—an increasing demand for AI professionals and a rapid growth in applications.

The Catalyst Path:
  • Pandemic hits
  • Remote work necessity
  • IT/AI investment surge
  • Comfort with AI grows
  • Labor bubble ignites

The Investment Surge

Three massive economic forces converged to create a huge talent scarcity relative to demand, sparking aggressive competitive hiring across the industry.

1 Venture Capital

Driven by uncertainty in traditional investments, VC funds pivoted to the massive opportunity in the IT market as a core necessity for enabling remote work.

2 Government Funding

Funding aimed at sustaining the economy during COVID accelerated. The Trump administration built upon Obama-era AI investments to put innovations to work making government better.

3 Research Coupling

Advancements in ML, deep learning, NLP, and generative AI in academia were suddenly coupled with immense commercial compute power and real-world testing opportunities.

Talent Scarcity & Competitive Hiring

The huge talent scarcity started competitive hiring. Companies needed people now, placing immense pressure on traditional recruitment channels. Meanwhile, remote work decoupled talent from geography.

People didn't necessarily want to work in the large cities where they traditionally had to live. They didn't feel tied to a given area anymore.

The 50-50 Fallacy

It is a fallacy to think the population you are drawing from to achieve gender equity is 50% men and 50% women. If only 20% of people entering the workforce are women, achieving a 50-50 representative workforce is mathematically extremely difficult.

"Unless you are creating a monopoly on the women coming into your organization, or potentially doing something perceived as reverse unfairness."

Why Pursue Diversity?

"It is not nearly as simple as people make it out to be."

  • Diverse Perspectives: Enhancing problem solving with different viewpoints.
  • Innate Differences: Taking advantage of potentially innate differences in leadership and task execution.
  • Fairness: Ensuring no one is unfairly denied opportunity based on gender or family situations.
  • Diverse Achievement: Recognizing excellence across different roles (e.g., treating sales excellence and delivery excellence fairly, despite different KPIs).

The Benchmark Reality

The actual workforce pipeline coming into the workforce is your realistic benchmark—not general population demographics.

  • Understand that you cannot simply create a monopoly on talent.
  • Look at metrics from a proper baseline perspective.
  • Think creatively about what makes sense in your organization.
  • Ask: What goals are you actually trying to achieve that are realistic?

Organizational Context Matters

What worked in one organization may not transfer directly to another. While the principles transfer (identify barriers, target interventions, measure outcomes), the specific programs must be customized. You must set realistic goals and analyze your own metrics from a proper baseline perspective.

Conclusion

The AI labor bubble taught us that talent markets are volatile, global, and unforgiving.

What begins as domestic scarcity can become global oversupply overnight when remote work erases geographic boundaries.

The diversity challenge teaches a complementary lesson: the talent pipeline is not what we assume it to be, and achieving equity requires understanding the actual numbers, identifying the real barriers, and designing science-based interventions that produce measurable results. From 18% to 38% representation with a 20% performance increase—that is what happens when you stop assuming and start measuring.

The AI labor market will continue to cycle through bubbles and corrections. Diversity will remain structurally challenging as long as the education pipeline produces lopsided outcomes. But organizations that understand both dynamics—the macroeconomic forces shaping who is available and the microeconomic programs that determine who thrives—will build the most capable, resilient, and equitable AI teams.

Benchmark against reality. Target actual barriers. Measure everything. Think creatively about what works in your context. The numbers do not lie, but they do require you to look at them honestly.

Dr. Ian McCullough, Johns Hopkins University
