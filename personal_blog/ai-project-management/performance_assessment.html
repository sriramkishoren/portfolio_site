B Opening Framing

Misaligned Goals — The Root of Conflict

Most organizational conflict is the result of misaligned goals, priorities, and incentives. People compete against different priorities — one person wants to upsell the client and generate more revenue, while another wants to deliver good quality and ensure the client feels well taken care of. Neither side is wrong. They have a difference of opinion on what is most important and what should garner the most resources, the attention of the boss, and the better praise on an assessment.

This leads to conflict — competing for resources, for time, for a limited pool of bonus money. One manager wants bonuses for their people, another manager wants them for theirs. It is not about the employee as much as it is about the tasks the employees were asked to do and which one the organization values more. Whenever you have misaligned goals, priorities, and incentives, you will find organizational conflict exists.

This insight reframes performance assessment as a strategic alignment challenge rather than merely a measurement exercise — the system itself becomes a mechanism for communicating and reinforcing what the organization truly values. When assessment criteria do not reflect organizational strategy, they create a gap between what leadership says it wants and what the incentive structure actually rewards.

To design an effective assessment system, we must first understand how strategy drives organizational structure.

D Organizational Model

Portfolio-Heavy Organizations — Account-Led Assessment

Some organizations are portfolio heavy. People manage portfolio campaigns, and the performance cycle is based on that portfolio — the account lead. The account lead is responsible for the profit and loss. They do the business development. They assess risk. They go after either wedge deals — sales growth on an existing client — or net new business.

They are primarily rating the people that are working on those accounts. People might be aligned to the AI practice or the IT practice, but their main role is client-facing, client-accounted, supporting that campaign.

Account Lead Responsibilities

Profit & Loss ownership, business development, risk assessment, wedge deals, net new business acquisition

Assessment Emphasis

Revenue generation, client satisfaction, delivery execution — the metrics that directly impact the account P&L

The risk is that technical depth, capability development, and cross-account knowledge sharing are undervalued because they do not directly appear in any single account's P&L.

Five Sources of Organizational Conflict

Performance assessment systems must account for these five distinct sources of conflict — each representing a different way misaligned incentives manifest in AI organizations. These conflicts are not failures; they are structural features of complex organizations.

1

Account Portfolio Competition

Resources that help a fellow portfolio create competition. Two computer vision people sharing code between accounts — now portfolio leads compete for biggest revenue or growth. Your boss asks: why are you helping another account instead of mine?

2

Overhead vs. Portfolio P&L Competition

The $75 million IRS AI deal — who gets credit for the P&L? The account lead with decades of relationships, or the AI specialist who brought the technical solution? A fundamental attribution challenge.

3

Intuition vs. Data-Driven Decisions

Malcolm Gladwell's "Blink" argues intuitive judgment is not necessarily inferior — it is a different way of making decisions. Sometimes data-driven organizations are better, other times intuition-driven organizations are better. No clear right answer.

4

Project vs. Technical Leadership

Schedule and milestones vs. solution quality. Should we delay a week to fix a major problem? Running over budget — where do we cut? Who gets to make that decision? This inevitably causes conflict.

5

Corporate Values vs. Profitability

There are issues between corporate values and profitability of an account. A ton of differences exist across the organization. When values require investment that reduces short-term profitability, which one does the assessment reward?

"If you do not get performance assessment right, you are going to leave people feeling undervalued, and they are going to quit. And they might be the people that you need the most."

G Conflict Deep Dive

Account Portfolio Competition — Resource Rivalry

If you lean heavily on portfolio leadership, then sharing resources creates competition. Two computer vision people share code between accounts. The other portfolio lead is in competition with your portfolio lead for who has the biggest revenue or the biggest growth. Your boss may ask: why are you spending time helping out another account? Why don't you help my account or the people that work for me?

Employee shares code/expertise across accounts — creates organizational value

But portfolio lead sees: time diverted from MY account — penalized for helping others

Performance assessment systems that do not account for cross-portfolio contribution create a rational incentive to hoard knowledge — the exact opposite of what AI organizations need.

I Conflict Deep Dive

Intuition vs. Data-Driven Decision Making

Intuition-Driven

Gut feeling and pattern recognition. Judgment based on experience. Some people across the organization are used to making decisions this way. If you are an intuitive person, you can be very threatened by the data organization.

Data-Driven

Metrics, analysis, and evidence. Decisions based on measurable outcomes. Data-driven leaders want quantifiable performance metrics like revenue generated, tickets closed, and delivery numbers.

Malcolm Gladwell's "Blink" makes a strong argument that intuitive judgment is not necessarily inferior to data-driven judgment — it is a different way of making decisions that might actually be better. No clear right answer — this naturally leads to competition.

In AI organizations, this tension is ironic: teams building data-driven systems may themselves be assessed by leaders who rely on intuition to evaluate their contributions.

Project vs. Technical Leadership — The Authority Question

There is a huge difference between project and technical leaders. In any project, there is going to be somebody maintaining the schedule and milestones, and then there is someone who really understands the core technology solution being developed. Who is in charge when they disagree?

Project Leader

  • › Maintaining the schedule and milestones
  • › Looking at the contract
  • › Ensuring all deliverables are hit
  • › Talking to the client, ensuring satisfaction
  • › People showing up and meeting daily goals

Technical Leader

  • › Understands the core technology solution
  • › Helps resolve or fix technical problems
  • › Responsible on the technical side
  • › Technical quality and solution architecture
  • › Technical integrity of the deliverable

When They Disagree

Scenario 1 — Quality vs. Schedule

Should we delay a week in delivering our deliverable to fix a major problem? Or do we keep to our schedule and make sure we hit the delivery milestones we are supposed to?

Scenario 2 — Budget Overrun

Running over budget — more time and more labor costs than expected. Where do we make cuts? Do we cut program leadership? Quality control? Do we just cut corners?

"Who gets to make that decision? This inevitably causes conflict."

In AI projects, this conflict is amplified because technical uncertainty is inherent — models may not converge, data quality issues emerge mid-project, and the path to a working solution is rarely linear, making rigid schedule adherence potentially counterproductive.

Performance assessment is not merely an HR exercise — it is the mechanism through which an organization communicates what it truly values. When goals, priorities, and incentives are misaligned, the assessment system becomes the battlefield where competing visions of organizational success collide.

Account leads compete for revenue credit. Capability practices compete for recognition of technical value. The question of who gets credit for a $75 million deal — the account relationship or the AI innovation — reveals the deep structural tensions that no simple rating form can resolve.

Intuitive leaders and data-driven leaders evaluate performance through different cognitive lenses, each valid in different contexts but incompatible when forced into a single framework. Project leaders and technical leaders disagree about whether to delay delivery to fix a problem or cut corners to hit a milestone — and the assessment system implicitly answers that question by what it rewards.

Corporate values and account profitability pull in opposite directions, and the assessment tells people which one actually matters. Get it right, and you align the organization. Get it wrong, and people feel undervalued. They quit. And they might be the people you need the most.

The organizations that build assessment systems aligned to their strategy, that account for the structural conflicts inherent in matrix operations, that recognize both portfolio delivery and capability development, and that make people feel their diverse contributions are seen and valued — those organizations will retain the talent that makes the difference. The ones that treat assessment as a compliance exercise will watch their best people walk out the door.
